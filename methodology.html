01 · What the model predicts

The target, precisely

Every modeling choice below follows from one deliberately narrow definition of "risk." Getting this exact wording right matters more than any metric on this page.

Target definition (quoted from the notebook)

The label is a new MODIS vegetation-fire hotspot candidate on the next day, in a given 0.25°×0.25° grid cell (roughly 17 miles/28 km across), after that cell has gone more than three consecutive days without a detection. It is not a guaranteed wildfire, a property-level risk, a fire-spread prediction, or an emergency warning — it is a same-day satellite-detectability signal, one step ahead.

  • Geography: continental United States (bounded by GridMET's own coverage)
  • Resolution: 0.25° grid cells, 1-day-ahead horizon
  • Training window: 2020–2023, calibrated on 2024, tested on 2025–2026

Why case-control sampling

New ignitions after a quiet spell are rare — well under 1% of all grid-cell-days. Training directly on that raw imbalance wastes most of a training pass on cells that never come close to igniting. The notebook instead builds a case-control sample: it keeps every positive (actual ignition) day and draws a much smaller, weighted sample of negative (quiet) days, then restores the true population rate at evaluation time with a sampling_weight column. The sampled training data shows roughly 17.3% positives, but the weights correctly imply a true nationwide prevalence of only about 0.6–0.7% — every held-out metric on this page is computed on that reweighted, population-correct basis, not the raw sampled rate.

02 · Features

25 features, five families

The model never sees a satellite image directly — it sees a row of 25 numbers per grid-cell-day, grouped here by what they describe.

🌪 Fire weather (8)

tmmx/tmmn (max/min temperature), pr (precipitation), vs (wind speed), rmin (minimum humidity), vpd (vapor pressure deficit), erc (energy release component — a standard fire-danger index), fm100 (100-hour dead fuel moisture). All from GridMET.

📅 Season (3)

month, plus sin_doy/cos_doy — day-of-year encoded on a circle so December 31st and January 1st read as neighbors, not as opposite ends of a number line.

🔥 Recent fire history (8)

Fire count and fire radiative power (frp), each looking back 1, 3, 7, and 14 days — whether this cell (or its recent past) has been actively burning nearby.

🏙 Human activity (4)

population_per_sq_km, log_population_density, primary_road_km_per_100_sq_km, distance_to_fire_station_km — people, roads, and emergency response reach.

📍 Location (2)

lat, lon — where the cell is. Deliberately tested in isolation below, since location alone can quietly encode "this is just a fire-prone place" without any real signal.

Does the model actually use every feature it's given?

A reviewer's critique of an earlier draft raised a concern common to raster/CNN wildfire-spread models: don't let the target or features hand the model a trivial shortcut instead of real fire-weather physics. This model has no image-mask component to concatenate, so that exact failure mode doesn't apply — but the underlying question is worth answering directly. The selected model's own permutation-importance table (Section 05 below) shows four features (fire_count_lag_1d, frp_lag_1d, frp_lag_3d, log_population_density) score exactly 0.000000 importance. This isn't a leakage bug — the decision day is always one day before the target day by construction, so fire_count_lag_1d is deterministically 0 for every positive row (a structural side effect of the >3-day quiet window in the target's own definition), and log_population_density is redundant with population_per_sq_km. To check this empirically rather than take the permutation-importance table's word for it, a fourth feature strategy, full_trimmed (21 features, these four dropped), was added to the candidate comparison below.

How the model was validated

Rather than a random row-by-row split — which would let the model "cheat" by seeing a cell's neighbors in training — each grid cell is assigned to a spatial block using a SHA-256 hash of its coordinates (stable_spatial_holdout), then whole blocks, not individual rows, are held out. 03 · Models compared

24 candidates, one winner

The notebook trains three model families — a gradient-boosted tree ensemble (hist_gradient_boosting), extremely randomized trees (extra_trees), and a random forest (random_forest) — against each of four feature strategies (full, no_coordinates, conditions_only, and full_trimmed — a leaner 21-feature variant testing the zero-importance audit above), each calibrated two ways (Platt/sigmoid and isotonic regression), for 24 total candidates. Every candidate is ranked by PR AUC (precision-recall area under the curve) on the calibration split — the right metric here, since ROC AUC is easy to inflate when positives are this rare.

The winner — hist_gradient_boosting, full feature set, sigmoid calibration — is only marginally ahead of the next few candidates. What matters more than the exact ranking is that gradient boosting on the full feature set beats every coordinate-restricted or alternative-algorithm candidate, consistently, across both calibration methods.

04 · Results

How well does the winning model actually do?

Real project results

Every metric, on every held-out split

Performance degrades gracefully from the easiest split (calibration, same years and places seen in spirit during training) to the hardest (spatial_test, both a later year and spatial blocks never seen in training) — the honest signature of a model that isn't overfitting to one place or one season.

Two ways to operate the model

A calibrated probability isn't a decision by itself — it needs a threshold. The notebook reports two named operating points: chase 80% recall (catch most real ignitions, accept a larger watchlist), or work within a fixed 5% alert budget (only ever flag the riskiest 5% of cell-days, whatever recall that buys you).

From ML metrics to operational cost

ROC AUC and PR AUC are useful for comparing candidates, but they mean little to an incident commander or a portfolio underwriter, and a single blended score like F1 quietly assumes a missed ignition and a false alarm cost the same — they don't. A missed ignition (a false negative) risks uncontained spread, property loss, and life safety. A false alarm (a false positive) risks an unnecessary patrol, inspection, or a premature precautionary action. That asymmetry, not a single accuracy number, is why this model ships two named operating points instead of one.

Neither threshold is "more correct" in an ML sense — both come from the exact same calibrated probability. Which one to operate on is a business decision about which kind of error is more expensive for a given user, not a modeling decision. 06 · Estimating economic loss

A separate model: what could a wildfire cost, if it causes reported damage at all?

Real project results

This is a deliberately separate consequence model, not a redefinition of the ignition target above. It uses NOAA/NCEI Storm Events bulk data for U.S. wildfire incidents, 2020–2026 — the same source behind the economic-loss map and address-lookup context on the prediction-model page. NOAA's damage figures are estimates, and a recorded zero can mean either "no monetary damage" or "damage never quantified," so the model uses a two-part hurdle design: (1) the probability an incident has any positive reported damage, times (2) the conditional loss amount when damage is reported. The result excludes indirect costs, smoke-related health costs, ecosystem services, business interruption, suppression costs, and unreported losses — it is a rough planning estimate, not an insurance valuation.

NOAA wildfire damage records, by year

Modeling choices

Only information plausibly known when an incident is first opened is used: state/forecast zone, month, and a small year trend. Final acreage, duration, fatalities, injuries, destroyed structures, and narrative text are deliberately excluded — those describe outcomes only known after the fact, and including them would leak the very consequence the model is trying to estimate in advance.

Does the extra complexity earn its keep?

Every split is also scored against baseline_state_average — a trivial hurdle model that just predicts each training-year state's historical average, with no weather, timing, or learned structure at all. If the real model (hurdle_rf_et, two 500-tree ensembles) can't beat that, its added complexity isn't earning its keep.

A metric that was fixed, and an interval that runs hot

An earlier version of this notebook combined predicted and observed loss in log1p space across every row, including the roughly 55% of rows with exactly $0 reported damage — since log1p(0) = 0 but the model's blended point estimate is never exactly zero, that metric was dominated by an artifact on zero-damage rows rather than real predictive skill (it reported values of 7–9, uninterpretable as a typical error). It has been replaced with dollar-space RMSE_usd_all, shown alongside median_absolute_error_usd above.

Built from the PTA wildfire-forecast project: NASA MODIS/FIRMS satellite detections (2020–2026), GridMET fire-weather variables, human activity data (population, roads, fire-station distance), and NIFC annual statistics.
